The routine · Mike Caulfield

Four moves before you believe or share

Professional fact-checkers barely read a doubtful page. They open new tabs and check what others say about it. Researchers call this lateral reading, and it beats careful reading of the page itself (Wineburg & McGrew, Stanford, 2019). SIFT turns it into four moves.

S

Stop

Pause before you read the comments or press Share. Misinformation runs on strong feelings: outrage, fear, or the pleasure of “I knew it!”. If a post hits you like that, take it as a signal to slow down.

I

Investigate the source

Find out who is behind it, outside the page itself. Design, a logo, an “About us” page and links to “studies” are all written by the same people, so they prove nothing.

  • Open a new tab and search the name of the outlet or author, with words like fact check, funding or Wikipedia.
  • Ask what they gain: money, politics, attention.
  • Look at the address: imposter sites copy real ones, like abcnews.com.co instead of abcnews.go.com.
F

Find better coverage

Search the claim itself and see who else reports it. A big event will be covered by several reliable outlets within hours.

Only one blog or a few anonymous accounts? Treat it as not confirmed yet. Real scoops also start in one place, so wait and check again rather than calling it fake.

T

Trace claims, quotes and media to the original

Content is often cut from its context to tell a different story.

  • Click the links: does the source really say what the headline claims?
  • Find the original number, quote or photo. Does it say the same thing there?
  • Check the date: old news often comes back as if it happened today.

Pictures and video

How to check images and video

What to checkHow to do it
Where it first appearedUpload the image, or a screenshot of a video frame, to Google Lens or TinEye. TinEye can sort results by oldest. Crop to the main part if nothing comes up. Same picture, other year or place? It is out of context.
Place and timeDo street signs, number plates, shop names, plants and weather match the claimed place and season?
AI-generated contentExtra fingers, broken text and odd shadows are no longer reliable tells: new generators fix them, and no glitch does not mean real. Check the origin instead: who posted it first, who could have filmed it, and whether reliable outlets show the same scene. If the file carries Content Credentials, they show how it was made.
AI-written textChatbots can invent quotes, studies and links. Trace every source an AI answer gives you before you repeat it.

Reasons to check

Five red flags

  1. ALL CAPS and pressure. “BREAKING!!!”, “SHARE BEFORE THIS IS DELETED!”
  2. Anonymous experts. “Scientists say…”, “Doctors are shocked…”, with no names or institutions.
  3. Screenshots instead of links. A screenshot of a post or headline takes seconds to fake and hides the original.
  4. “They won’t show you this”. Claims that the media hide a topic that is easy to find.
  5. Headline and text don’t match. The article itself admits the headline isn’t quite true.

A red flag is a reason to check, not proof. A polished page can be fake, and a clumsy one can be true.

Beyond fakes

True news has a frame too

A story can mislead without a single false fact: through the headline word, who is quoted first, the photo, the numbers (“80% fat-free” or “contains 20% fat”) and what is left out. Five questions help you see the frame.

01

Who made it?

Who owns it, and why now? News, opinion or ad?

02

How is it made?

Headline, sources, words, photo, placement.

03

Who is shown how?

Who is “us” and who is “them”?

04

Who is it for?

What should you feel, and then do?

05 · strongest

What’s missing?

Which numbers, voices, context?

Honest frame

The position is visible: opinion is labelled, sources are named, the other side is quoted.

Manipulation

The position hides as fact: one side only, emotional words presented as news, missing context.

Pocket version

Before you share

  • Stop. I haven’t shared or believed it yet.
  • Source. I checked who is behind it in a new tab, not on its “About us” page.
  • Interest. I know whether they gain money, votes or attention from it.
  • Coverage. Other reliable sources say the same, the opposite, or nothing.
  • Original. I found the original number, quote or photo, and it says the same.
  • Picture. I ran it through a reverse image search and know where and when it first appeared.
